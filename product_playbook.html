The Eric Product Playbook

Building products that remove unnecessary complexity so people can focus on the work that matters most.

Introduction

My product approach was shaped where software meets real-world pressure.

Healthcare operations, fulfilment systems, frontline workflows, and platform work taught me that product management is not only about feature delivery. It is about understanding the work people are trying to do, the decisions they make under pressure, and the operating environment that determines whether a product can be trusted.

Product Principles

Principles I use when product work touches operational complexity.

Observe before designing.

Direct observation prevents teams from designing around an imagined workflow.

Mission-driven teams normalise inefficiency.

Strong teams often absorb broken processes because the mission matters. Product should make that hidden effort visible.

Good products reduce cognitive load, not only clicks.

The goal is not only fewer steps. It is less mental math, less uncertainty, and better decisions.

Automate repetitive calculation while preserving valuable human judgement.

Automation should remove routine calculation without removing the expert user from the decision.

Every override is product feedback.

When users override a recommendation, the product should learn what the system did not understand.

Adoption starts during discovery, not launch.

Users are more likely to trust a product when they helped shape the workflow before release.

Data informs decisions; people make them.

Metrics should sharpen judgment, not replace context.

The operating environment is a product requirement.

Connectivity, devices, staffing, incentives, and training decide whether a product works in practice.

Discovery Framework

Questions that keep discovery grounded.

  • What job is the user actually trying to accomplish?
  • What decisions are they making?
  • Where does cognitive load exist?
  • What workarounds have they normalised?
  • What assumptions are we making?
  • Can the user’s real environment support the proposed solution?
  • How will success be measured?

Product Operating Model

A simple loop for turning operational reality into product decisions.

  1. Observe
  2. Define
  3. Validate
  4. Prioritise
  5. Build collaboratively
  6. Release gradually
  7. Measure
  8. Learn

Human-in-the-loop Model

Automate calculation without removing expert judgement.

The goal is to automate repetitive calculation without removing expert judgement. The best system recommends, explains, captures overrides, and improves from patterns.

  1. System recommendation
  2. User review
  3. Accept or override
  4. Structured override reason
  5. Pattern analysis
  6. Improved recommendation

Evidence From Practice

How these principles show up in real work.

A. Automated Slotting

From full automation to decision support with human control.

Discovery across 8 of 13 distribution centres in Ghana, Rwanda, Kenya and Nigeria showed that a fully automated queue was not the right first product. The better product was decision support: a recommendation engine that reduced manual calculation while preserving operator control.

  • The slotting logic used order priority, order time, package metadata, facility ETA, available drone bodies, batteries and wings, and the maximum number of drones permitted in flight simultaneously.
  • Emergency orders could move ahead of the queue.
  • Operators could override recommendations when real-world conditions changed.
  • Override reasons included incoming emergency order, changed order priority, after-hours delivery and reduced capacity caused by a faulty LRU.
  • Override reasons were selected from a predefined list developed with users.

Weekly on-time delivery improved from 77% to 97%, 3-4 minutes were saved per large order, and target users reached 100% adoption within four weeks.

B. Flight Tracker Integration

Connecting isolated systems so operators could stop copying operational context by hand.

The Fulfilment System and Flight Tracking System were originally isolated. Operators manually transferred identifiers and operational information between systems, which added delay and created unnecessary room for error.

  • The integration connected Order IDs, Shipment IDs, Package IDs, Facility IDs, drone identifiers and Flight IDs.
  • Engineering observed the workflow live before PRDs and RFCs were finalised.
  • Reliability measures included duplicate protection, reconciliation logic, Sentry monitoring, on-call alerts, staging tests, feature flags and staged rollout.
  • Combined workflow improvements reduced average order-processing time from approximately 7 minutes to under 1.5 minutes.
  • Most of that improvement came from the Flight Tracker integration, with automated customer messaging contributing additional savings.
  • The metric was measured using system timestamps.

C. Ordering Portal Failure

The experience that changed how I validate product assumptions.

Approximately 60% of orders were placed through WhatsApp. I proposed a self-service ordering portal to reduce manual order intake, and the concept received strong apparent buy-in. The team spent approximately six weeks building it.

After launch, out of roughly 600 facilities, only two used it. Key assumptions about active email accounts, laptops and reliable internet were not validated. The lesson was not that the team lacked effort. The lesson was that problem validation, solution validation, and operating-environment validation are separate product risks.
